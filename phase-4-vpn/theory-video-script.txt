SLIDE 1 — TITLE
[CUE: "Phase 4 — Dual-Firewall Network & a Site-to-Site VPN"]

"This is Phase 4 of the SOC lab, and it's the last one. Phase 1 asked:
can we see? Phase 2: can we see across a router? Phase 3: can we still
trust what we see once a firewall rewrites addresses? Phase 4 asks the
hardest question of all — we encrypt the traffic, so the SOC can no
longer read it on the wire, and we ask: can we still detect the attack?

This video is the theory. A second video will walk through building it
and running the attacks live."


SLIDE 2 — OBJECTIVES
[CUE: "What Phase 4 does" — four points]

"Four things happen in this phase. We add a second site — a branch
office behind its own firewall. We join the two sites with an encrypted
IPsec VPN across an untrusted transit. We carry the branch's traffic
back to the central SOC, the way a real remote office reports home. And
then we attack — from inside the branch, and from on the wire in the
middle — and we see where the SOC can still catch it.

That's the whole through-line of the lab in one sentence: can we see,
across a router, once a firewall lies, and now — once the traffic is
encrypted."


SLIDE 3 — THE CORE CHANGE
[CUE: routed link vs encrypted VPN, two cards]

"So what actually changes. On the left is what we had: a routed link. It
moves packets between networks, and anyone on the path can read every
packet — the ports, the flags, the payload, all in the clear. Our sensor
sees the traffic directly.

On the right is the VPN. It wraps each packet inside an encrypted one.
Now anyone on the path sees only an ESP blob between two firewalls. The
ports, the flags, the payload — all sealed inside.

A VPN is just a routed link that first seals every packet. It protects
that packet from the wire — and, as a side effect, it hides it from our
own defenders."


SLIDE 4 — TOPOLOGY
[CUE: Site A ↔ Transit ↔ Site B diagram]

"Here's the layout with real addresses. On the left, Site A — the SOC.
It still has its Intranet, ten-ten-ten, and its DMZ, ten-ten-twenty,
with all the monitoring tools and the sensor tap.

On the right, Site B — the branch — on ten-ten-thirty, behind its own
firewall, with an attacker inside it.

In the middle is the transit, ten-ten-zero. That's the untrusted
segment — it stands in for the internet — and it carries the two
firewall WAN addresses, a passive router, and a seat for an on-path
attacker.

The dashed line is the IPsec tunnel. It joins the two LANs, and it is
the only path a branch host takes to reach the SOC — crossing that
untrusted middle sealed."


SLIDE 5 — THE IP TABLE
[CUE: addressing table, two columns]

"The full addressing. Site A on the left: the firewall fw-a with its
three legs, the SIEM on ten-ten-ten-twenty, Grafana on twenty-four, the
client, and the sensor tap. Transit and Site B on the right: the two
firewall WAN addresses that terminate the tunnel — ten-ten-zero-one and
ten-ten-zero-two — the transit router, the on-path attacker seat, and
the branch firewall fw-b with its attacker on ten-ten-thirty-forty.

The tunnel joins ten-ten-ten-zero and ten-ten-thirty-zero, using IKEv2
and AES-GCM. And, as in every phase, management is out-of-band on the
one-ninety-two network, so admin never touches the traffic we watch."


SLIDE 6 — THE TECHNOLOGIES
[CUE: four cards — two firewalls, transit, the tunnel, tools]

"The pieces that build it. Two firewalls — both OPNsense: fw-a at the
SOC and fw-b at the branch. Each is stateful, and each terminates one
end of the tunnel and enforces its own site's policy.

The transit is a VyOS router, playing the part of the internet — the
untrusted segment the tunnel has to cross.

The tunnel itself is IPsec, built with strongSwan: IKEv2 for the key
exchange, AES-GCM for the encryption, and a pre-shared key for
authentication.

And the detection stack is the same one we've built all along — Suricata
and Zeek on the sensor, Wazuh, Grafana — plus nmap and Nikto as the
attacker's tools. One new idea, three new parts: a second firewall, an
untrusted transit, and an encrypted tunnel between them."


SLIDE 7 — HOW THE TUNNEL WORKS
[CUE: IKE + ESP, the swanctl summary block]

"How the tunnel actually comes up. There are two stages. IKE is the
handshake: the two firewalls authenticate each other with the shared
key, and agree on secret keys. That's done once, and refreshed on a
timer. Then ESP carries the traffic: every real packet is encrypted with
those keys and wrapped as ESP. That's the continuous flow.

It runs in tunnel mode, which means the whole original packet is sealed
inside — only the firewall-to-firewall header shows on the outside.

Two things have to line up or it simply won't come up: both ends must
agree on identity, on the key, and on the exact pair of networks. And
one detail that trips everyone — ESP is a protocol, number fifty, not a
port. So the firewall has to be told to permit the protocol, not a port
number."


SLIDE 8 — THE CORE QUESTION
[CUE: "Encryption hides the attack from everyone — including your defenders"]

"Now the problem this phase exists for. Encryption is doing exactly what
it's supposed to — but it hides the attack from everyone, including us.

Our detection engine works by matching ports, flags, and payloads.
Inside the tunnel there are none of those to read — just an opaque ESP
blob between two firewalls.

So the question is simple to say and hard to answer: how do you catch an
attacker you cannot read?"


SLIDE 9 — THE ANSWER: TWO VIEWS
[CUE: two cards — on the transit vs at the decryption point]

"The answer is that where you watch matters more than ever, and there
are two very different places to watch.

On the transit — in the middle — you get only ESP. No ports, no flags,
no payload. Content inspection is dead. All you have left is traffic
analysis: the timing, the size, the regularity. You learn that something
happened, not what.

At the decryption point — inside the site — it's the opposite. Once fw-a
decrypts the tunnel, the packets are in the clear again. Signatures
work. The sensor reads the whole attack, and it names the real source —
because traffic between the two sites inside the tunnel is not
NAT'd, so attribution survives.

The blindness lives only inside the tunnel. Detection just moves to where
the firewall hands the traffic back in the clear."


SLIDE 10 — TWO ATTACKERS
[CUE: two cards — branch attacker vs transit attacker]

"To test both halves of that, we use two attackers.

The first is inside Branch B, on ten-ten-thirty-forty. It rides the
trusted VPN straight into the SOC. Because it's decrypted at
headquarters, it is detectable — with the real branch IP named. This one
proves a VPN does not vet a threat that comes from a trusted branch.

The second sits on the transit — the on-path attacker. It scans the
tunnel from the wire and sees only ESP. This one proves the opposite:
that the VPN's confidentiality holds, and the man-in-the-middle is blind.

One attacker tests what the VPN lets through; the other tests what the
VPN hides."


SLIDE 11 — CAUGHT AT THE DECRYPTION POINT
[CUE: nmap + Nikto evidence, real source .30.40]

"Here's the first attacker's result. From the branch, we ran an nmap
scan and a Nikto web scan against the SOC. Both rode the tunnel, were
decrypted at fw-a, and lit up the Site A sensor.

The nmap scan fired our SYN-scan and sweep rules. The Nikto scan fired
the local-file-inclusion rule we wrote back in Phase 3 — six times — plus
a known web-app signature.

And look at the source on every one of them: ten-ten-thirty-forty. The
real branch attacker. A signature we wrote two phases ago just caught a
scanner that arrived fully encrypted — because by the time the sensor saw
it, the firewall had already decrypted it."


SLIDE 12 — BLIND ON THE WIRE
[CUE: transit capture — ESP only]

"Now the second attacker's view — the exact same scan, captured on the
transit at the same moment.

All you see is ESP: encrypted blobs going between ten-ten-zero-two and
ten-ten-zero-one. No ports. No flags. No ten-ten-thirty-forty. No scan
pattern at all. Only two firewalls exchanging sealed envelopes.

From the attacker's own seat on the wire, the scan is invisible. This is
the confidentiality of the VPN, proven from the inside."


SLIDE 13 — CORRELATION
[CUE: two-row table — transit vs sensor — aligned by time]

"So we have two witnesses to one attack, and each one only knows half of
it.

The transit knows when the firewalls exchanged a burst of encrypted
traffic, and how much — but not what it was. The Site A sensor knows who
and what — the branch attacker scanning an internal host — but not that
it crossed an untrusted wire.

Neither one alone tells the story. But line them up by timestamp, and the
encrypted surge on the wire is the same scan the sensor decoded a moment
later. Put together, the branch attacker who was hidden inside the tunnel
is fully unmasked.

That's the lesson: once traffic is encrypted, no single log is
authoritative. Correlating across the boundary is the skill this phase
teaches."


SLIDE 14 — VERDICT
[CUE: "PHASE 4 — PASSED"]

"So, Phase 4 — passed.

We joined two sites with a real encrypted tunnel. We caught an attacker
who came in through a trusted branch, and named them, at the point where
the firewall decrypts. We watched the same attack out on the wire and saw
nothing but ESP. And we unmasked the attacker by correlating the two
views.

The phase in one line: encryption blinds the middle; the decryption point
restores sight; correlation names the attacker.

That completes the four-phase lab. The next video is the practical part —
building this tunnel and running these attacks live. Thanks for watching."
