SCENE 1 — OPENING
[CUE: VirtualBox manager, both firewalls + branch attacker + SOC VMs running]

"In the theory video, I explained how Phase 4 is designed — two sites
joined by an encrypted VPN. Now I'm going to show that it actually works.

Everything is running from the final Phase 4 snapshot. I'll bring up the
tunnel, attack the SOC from inside the branch, and show two things at once:
the attack is caught inside the site, and on the wire in the middle it is
completely invisible."


SCENE 2 — TOPOLOGY
[CUE: two-site diagram]

"Quickly, the layout.

There are two firewalls now. On the left, Site A — the SOC — with the
Intranet ten-ten-ten and the DMZ ten-ten-twenty. On the right, Site B —
a branch office — on ten-ten-thirty.

Between them is the transit, ten-ten-zero. That's the untrusted middle —
it stands in for the internet, and an attacker sits on it.

The two firewalls are joined by an IPsec tunnel. Branch traffic to the SOC
only travels one way: sealed inside that tunnel across the transit.

Keep one point in mind: the network sensor sits inside Site A, where the
firewall hands the traffic back in the clear. That location is the whole
story of this phase."


SCENE 3 — THE TUNNEL IS UP
[CUE: fw-a shell — swanctl --list-sas]

"First let me confirm the tunnel is established.

On the SOC firewall I list the security associations. The connection to the
branch firewall, ten-ten-zero-two, shows ESTABLISHED, and the child shows
INSTALLED. So the encrypted tunnel between the two LANs is live."

[CUE: branch attacker — ping -c4 10.10.10.50 → TTL 62]
"From the branch attacker I ping a host inside the SOC. It replies, and the
TTL is sixty-two. That drop tells me the packet passed through two
firewalls — it really did cross the tunnel and come back."


SCENE 4 — ATTACK 1: NMAP OVER THE VPN
[CUE: branch attacker — nmap -sS 10.10.10.50]

"Now the attack. I'm inside the branch, and I run a SYN scan against a host
in the SOC.

This traffic leaves the branch, gets encrypted by the branch firewall,
crosses the transit as ESP, and is decrypted by the SOC firewall before it
reaches the target."

[CUE: Site A sensor — eve.json alerts, src 10.10.30.40]
"And on the SOC sensor, the scan is detected. My own nmap rules fire — the
SYN scan and the connect sweep.

Look at the source address: ten-ten-thirty-forty. That's the real branch
attacker. Traffic between the two sites inside the tunnel isn't NAT'd, so
even though it arrived encrypted, once it's decrypted the sensor names the
true source. Attribution survives the VPN."


SCENE 5 — ATTACK 2: NIKTO OVER THE VPN
[CUE: branch attacker — nikto -h http://10.10.10.50]

"Next a web scan. From the same branch machine I run Nikto against a web
service in the SOC."

[CUE: Site A sensor — LAB LFI attempts + ET signature, src 10.10.30.40]
"The sensor lights up again. It catches the path-traversal attempts — the
scanner asking for the etc-passwd file — using the custom rule I wrote back
in Phase 3, plus a known web-app signature.

The important part is the same: a signature I wrote two phases ago just
caught an attacker who arrived fully encrypted, because by the time the
sensor saw the packets, the firewall had already decrypted them."


SCENE 6 — THE SAME ATTACK, BLIND ON THE WIRE
[CUE: transit tap / KALI — tcpdump -nei <if> esp, during the scan]

"Now the other half. This is the exact same scan, but watched from the
transit — the untrusted middle, where a man-in-the-middle attacker sits.

All I can capture is ESP: encrypted blobs going between ten-ten-zero-two and
ten-ten-zero-one, the two firewall addresses.

There are no ports. No flags. No ten-ten-thirty-forty. No scan pattern at
all. From here, the attacker can tell that the two firewalls are talking, and
roughly when and how much — and absolutely nothing about what's inside.

That's the confidentiality of the VPN, proven from the attacker's own seat."


SCENE 7 — CORRELATION / THE UNMASK
[CUE: two-row table]
   Transit (on-path)  →  encrypted surge  fw-b ⇄ fw-a   — when + how much
   Site A sensor      →  10.10.30.40 → 10.10.10.50       — who + what

"So I have two witnesses to one event, and each only knows half.

The transit knows WHEN the firewalls exchanged a burst of encrypted traffic,
and how much — but not what it was.

The SOC sensor knows WHO and WHAT — the branch attacker scanning an internal
host — but not that it crossed an untrusted wire.

I line them up by timestamp. The encrypted surge on the transit is the same
scan the sensor decoded a moment later inside the site. Put together, the
branch attacker who was hidden inside the tunnel is fully unmasked.

Once traffic is encrypted, no single log tells the whole story. Correlating
the encrypted flow on the wire with the decrypted detection inside the site
is exactly the skill this phase is about."


SCENE 8 — CLOSE
[CUE: Grafana — Site A alert timeline]

"That completes Phase 4.

We built two firewalls and a real encrypted site-to-site VPN. We attacked the
SOC from a trusted branch and caught it — with the real source named — at the
point where the firewall decrypts. And we proved that the same attack, out on
the transit, is completely blind: only ESP.

The lesson of the phase in one line: encryption blinds the middle, the
decryption point restores sight, and correlation names the attacker. Thanks
for watching."
